01The record

It writes down what it said, then checks.

Each verdict is logged the moment it changes, stamped with the price and the index, and marked on an anniversary that cannot be moved afterwards. A tool that flatters itself is worse than no tool, because it launders a guess into a number.

Try it — make a call on real history

Three years of real daily closes, ticker hidden. Call it, set a horizon, and watch the mark land against the S&P — the same way the terminal marks every verdict it issues.

What this is aiming at, in the only terms that can be checked

The target

Positive expectancy per call, measured against the index, with a 95% interval that clears zero. Not a win rate and not a return. A return target tells you nothing about whether it is reachable, because you picked the answer before you had the inputs.

The constraint

A stated ruin tolerance that sizing is never allowed to breach. Three limits bind every position — policy cap, volatility budget, distance to stop — and the smallest one wins. Being right about the company and wrong about the size is how accounts end.

The threshold

The record counts as evidence when the arithmetic says so, not at a round number. The calls needed to separate a real edge from luck is (1.96 × dispersion ÷ edge)² — roughly 138 at a two-point edge with twelve-point dispersion. The terminal computes it from your own marks as they accumulate and shows the gap. Below it, the interval straddles zero almost regardless of the point estimate, and the honest reading is don't know.

Why it is put this way: a hit rate is the most manufacturable statistic in finance. Sell covered calls, take profit at +0.5%, never stop out, hold the losers to recovery, and you can report being right nine times in ten while losing money on every third gap-down — 80% of wins at +1% against 20% of losses at −10% is an expectancy of −1.2% per trade. Right nearly always, and reliably bankrupt. Expectancy cannot be dressed up that way, because the large loss stays in the average.

03The terminal

Fourteen screens. One question each.

Every screen answers one decision and shows the arithmetic behind it. Nothing is a black box, because a system nobody can audit is a system nobody should act on.

Command

One list each morning: what needs you today. Sell signals, breached limits, stops hit, reviews due, idle cash. Most days it reports that nothing needs you, and that is the correct answer.

Scorecard

Every verdict the system has issued, marked against the index at 30, 90, 180 and 365 days. Reported with a confidence interval, so a thin sample reads as a thin sample rather than a result.

Sizing

Three limits before any trade — policy cap, volatility budget, distance to stop — and the smallest one binds. Being right about the company and wrong about the size is how accounts end.

Drawdown

Not where the book might finish but the hole it passes through. Sampled from real market history rather than a normal curve, because losses arrive in runs and a bell curve cannot represent that.

Supply map

Who a company depends on, upstream and down. Five holdings that all rely on one supplier are one position, not five, and no other screen will surface it.

Behaviour

How long positions are actually held. Whether what gets sold keeps rising without us. Whether the cash rule survives contact with a bad week. The one dataset that cannot be bought.

Read the refusal list
The rule that governs this list

No further analytics are added until the call scorecard holds enough marks for its own arithmetic to clear zero — a figure the terminal derives from the observed edge and dispersion rather than asserting, and which is nearer 138 than the 40 first guessed here. Evidence arrives at one day per day and no amount of building accelerates it, so every module added before then would change the interface without changing what is known. Most of the list is risk machinery — it exists to make being wrong survivable, not to make being right more frequent, and treating it as the second thing is the most common error made with it.

05Questions

The ones worth answering.

Is this available to the public?

No. PerceptFolio is internal infrastructure operated by NorthBridge's financial branch. It is not sold, licensed or offered as a service. This page exists so that people who work here have one place to sign in from.

Is any of this investment advice?

No. The terminal applies rules its operator configures to public data and shows the arithmetic. When it says BUY, that means the position cleared the bar that operator set. It has no view on anyone's circumstances, makes no recommendation, and takes no custody. Judgement stays with the person at the keyboard.

Does it promise to beat the market?

It promises nothing about returns, and you will not find a performance figure anywhere in this product. What it does is measure — every call marked against the index on a fixed horizon, reported with a confidence interval wide enough to show when the sample is too thin to mean anything. A tool that tells you it wins is selling. A tool that tells you how often it was wrong is working.

Where does my data live?

In your own browser. Holdings, prices, notes and history are stored locally on the device you use. With sync enabled it goes to a private store controlled by NorthBridge, protected by a key held per device. There is no central database holding anyone's book.

Why is my scorecard empty when I start?

Because a fixed-horizon record cannot be faked backwards. To know what a stock did ninety days after a call, something has to look on the day. The terminal marks each call on its own anniversary going forward, so the first meaningful reading is roughly a quarter out.

Does it place trades?

No, and it never will. There is no broker connection and none is planned. It reports what the rules flagged and shows the numbers; execution happens in your own brokerage, by you.
